**Proposed title:** Surface effects of sudden stratospheric warmings are a common
shift, not a property of the event

**Summary for the editors**

Forecasts initialised at a sudden stratospheric warming (SSW) are more skilful
than forecasts initialised at other times — a result this journal published in
2013 (Sigmond et al., *Nat. Geosci.* 6, 98). The field's explanation of that skill
is event-specific: roughly two thirds of SSWs are said to "propagate downward" to
the surface and the rest not, and current work asks which events will couple and
whether forecast systems can tell in advance.

We test that explanation in a designed experiment. In the SNAPSI multi-model
ensembles the stratosphere of every member is nudged to the same observed SSW, so
any difference between "downward" and "non-downward" members cannot come from
the event. Across nine forecast models we find that the SSW shifts the surface
distribution by about one standard deviation without changing its shape; that
the downward/non-downward contrast is the same with the SSW imposed as with no
SSW at all — the value a threshold produces on noise; that in 1,888 simulated
events an SSW adds no out-of-sample predictability over an ordinary winter day;
and that the field's two archetype events (February 2018, "propagating"; January
2019, "not") received the same forced odds and differ by ordinary member-to-member
noise.

**Why Nature Geoscience**

The result locates the source of the skill documented in this journal: it is the
common shift, and it is captured by a shifted probability distribution rather
than by a prediction of which events will couple. That changes what extended-range
forecasts after an SSW should state, and how "non-downward" outcomes — currently
treated as forecast busts — should be read. It speaks to the subseasonal
prediction community as a whole, not only to specialists in stratosphere–
troposphere coupling.

**What is new relative to prior work**

Earlier work noted that downward/non-downward differences are partly built in by
the classification (White et al., *J. Clim.* 2019) and argued for a continuum
(Coughlin & Gray 2009). We provide the experimental test, the quantification of
predictability against a size-matched null, and the direct test of the archetype
pair.

**Limitations we state openly**

The evidence is primarily model-based (the observational record, 42 usable
events, is underpowered for these questions, though every observational test
agrees); SNAPSI contains two Northern Hemisphere events; the Southern Hemisphere
case is a minor warming.
