# Cover letter — Nature Geoscience, Article

[[AUTHORS: date, names, affiliations, corresponding author]]

Dear Editor,

We submit "Imposed stratospheric warmings change the odds of surface impacts, not the class contrast" for
consideration as an Article.

**The question.** When does a contrast between classes of climate impacts reveal a difference in forcing?
Geoscience routinely sorts extremes into classes (El Niño flavours, weather regimes, climatic epochs) and reads the
contrast between them as a difference in what drives them. When the classes are cut from the outcome itself, the
cut alone produces a contrast, whether or not the forcing differs. In seismology, an earthquake is a foreshock only
once a larger one follows, and the apparently special properties of foreshocks turned out to arise from one
triggering process acting on all earthquakes (Helmstetter & Sornette, JGR 2003). We ask the same question by
intervention for the most widely used impact classification in stratosphere–troposphere research: does the class
contrast move when the forcing does?

**The answer in one unit (Fig. 1d).** Sudden stratospheric warmings (SSWs) precede weeks of cold weather over
Europe and Asia; in the UK alone they have been linked to about 620 extra deaths per event. They are sorted into
the roughly two thirds whose influence "propagates downward" and the rest, by a label defined from the surface
outcome. In an experiment that imposes the same observed SSW on every ensemble member of nine models, the
forcing moves the mean surface annular mode by −1.36 standard deviations and the contrast between downward and
non-downward members by −0.03 [−0.12, +0.05]. The share of downward outcomes rises from 45% to 84%, in all 36
model–initialisation pairs. The forcing changes the odds; the contrast is set by the threshold and the spread of
one population.

**Why the result is general, not a special case.**
- **Where the forcing saturates the class.** In the 11 ensembles where the forcing leaves almost no non-downward
  members, a registered test displaces the members back by the imposed effect. It is inconclusive by its own
  tolerance; an analysis added afterwards finds the departure to be what their narrower spread predicts (+0.03
  [−0.03, +0.06] standard deviations net of spread). The contrast does not grow with the size of the forcing.
- **A different hemisphere and event type.** In a Southern Hemisphere warming that also widened the distribution,
  the contrast grew by what one widened population produces (registered test).
- **A variable the label does not use.** The classes' difference in northern-Eurasian temperature is reproduced
  by a matched null: in the experiment, inside a tolerance fixed in advance. The residual is −0.15 K in point
  estimate, and the registered test would detect 0.5 standard deviations of temperature. In observations the null
  matches in point estimate.
- **Observations without any model.** Event-free dates displaced by the SSW shift reproduce the observed "two
  thirds", and event-free dates reproduce 97% of the class contrast even without the shift. With the shift
  estimated from other winters, the null predicts held-out events better than climatology (continuous ranked
  probability score gain 0.19 [0.07, 0.30]).
- **A test on events that have not happened.** Forecasts for every possible onset day were committed before the
  coming winter. They will be scored on every Northern Hemisphere SSW until 2036 under a rule fixed in advance.

**Why it matters beyond the stratosphere.** A class defined on the outcome it is meant to explain always shows a
contrast in that outcome (Altman & Royston, BMJ 2006). The same reasoning runs through attribution debates, such
as whether a particular SSW "caused" the February 2021 Texas cold outbreak (Cohen et al., Science 2021; Davis et
al., Nature Communications 2022). We supply an experimental test that any outcome-defined typology can be put
through: does the class contrast move when the forcing does? We also give a practical rule (Table 1). The
classification stays legitimate for describing outcomes and as a forecast target, because its rate responds to
the forcing. A realised label, or the contrast between classes, is not evidence of how strongly the forcing
acted. We do not claim that distinct response types are absent, and we state what evidence would show them.

**What is new relative to earlier work.**
- **Split and displaced vortices.** That typology was shown to differ little at the surface (Maycock & Hitchcock 2015);
  the downward label differs in kind, because it is defined on the outcome.
- **White et al. (2019).** They showed in one model that the classes differ before onset. We add:
  - an intervention that changes the forcing;
  - a null that reproduces the observed rate, contrast and regional difference;
  - its skill on held-out winters;
  - independence from how the criterion is set.
- **Lu & Rao (2026)** describe contrasts among subtypes of downward events without aiming to establish causes; we
  test the separate inference from a class contrast to a difference in forcing.

**What we report against our own expectations.** Every test is listed with its registration status (Extended
Data Table 1), and designs were committed before their runs. Results that went against us are reported as limits:
- **Operational under-prediction.** The under-prediction of the shift by operational forecasts did not recur
  after four later events.
- **Model-derived cold-risk probability.** It over-predicted after twelve held-out SSWs of 1941–1958.
- **Event-conditioned responses.** They differ substantially between events, and how much of that the
  stratosphere forces is not settled.
- **Strongly forced ensembles.** The registered back-shift test there is inconclusive by its own tolerance; the
  spread analysis that explains it was added afterwards, and we say so.
- **A third intervention.** The only public one (CESM2 forecasts of the January 2021 SSW, 21 members per arm) is
  too small to decide; we report it as such.
