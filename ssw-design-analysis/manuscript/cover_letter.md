# Cover letter — Nature Geoscience, Article

[[AUTHORS: date, names, affiliations, corresponding author]]

Dear Editor,

We submit "Stratospheric warmings shift winter cold risk; the downward-propagation label does not reveal two kinds of event" for
consideration as an Article.

**The question.** Earth-system extremes are often understood by sorting them into kinds: El Niño flavours,
weather regimes, climatic epochs. Some typologies have survived scrutiny. Others have proved consistent with a
single regime or with stochastic variability (Stephenson et al., QJRMS 2004; Neukom et al., Nature 2019), or
reproducible as a continuum (Thual & Dewitte, Nature Geoscience 2023).

Sudden stratospheric warmings (SSWs) are a case with direct stakes. They precede weeks of cold, blocked weather
over Europe and Asia; in the UK alone they have been linked to about 620 extra deaths per event. Research and
forecasting sort them into the roughly two thirds whose influence "propagates downward" to the surface and the
rest. The label is defined from the surface outcome it is then used to explain, and its users recognise that
part of the class difference is built in by that definition. What has not been measured is how much: whether
an imposed stratospheric change alters the class contrast or only the class rate, and whether a null containing
no event reproduces the contrast and predicts held-out events.

**What we show.**
- **One shifted distribution.** With an experiment that imposes the same observed SSW on every ensemble member in
  nine models, the label behaves as a threshold on one shifted distribution. The SSW changes the share of
  downward outcomes from 45% to 84%, but not the class contrast; averaged over models and events it
  translates the distribution within a tolerance declared before the test (single events and regional
  temperature remain unresolved).
- **Two thirds from one population.** Observed event-free dates, shifted by the measured effect, reproduce both
  the "two thirds" and the class contrast, across 108 versions of the criterion and with the shift estimated
  from other winters.
- **No resolved step at the SSW threshold.** Across all observed vortex weakenings since 1940, in two
  reanalyses, the surface response grows steadily with how far the wind falls, with no resolved step where it
  reverses (observations exclude only steps about as large as the whole SSW effect; CMIP6, 15% of it).
- **Little gain from knowing the event, in the forecasts tested.** In 1,517 simulated SSWs, event information
  adds about 1% to probabilistic skill beyond the shift, less than on ordinary winter days.
- **No added skill from the SSW label in a state forecast.** Across 57 observed SSWs since 1940, adding an SSW
  indicator to a statistical forecast from the continuous state at issue time did not improve it.

**Why it matters beyond the stratosphere.** A class defined on the outcome it is meant to predict always shows
a contrast in that outcome. That is a general trap (Altman & Royston, BMJ 2006), and it also shapes attribution
debates: whether a particular SSW "caused" the February 2021 Texas cold outbreak is argued in both directions
(Cohen et al., Science 2021; Davis et al., Nature Communications 2022). Our results say the answerable question
is how far the warming shifted the odds. For forecasting, the product is a calibrated probability (of the
regional variable, of a negative annular mode, or of the label itself); what a realised label cannot carry is
evidence of how strongly the stratosphere acted in that event. We do not claim that distinct response types are
absent; we show that the realised labels cannot identify them.

**What is new relative to earlier work.**
- **Split and displaced vortices.** That typology was shown to differ little at the surface (Maycock &
  Hitchcock 2015). The downward label is different in kind: it is defined on the outcome.
- **White et al. (2019).** They showed, in one model, that the two classes already differ before onset. We go
  further:
  - we test the label with an intervention that fixes the forcing;
  - we reproduce the observed rate and contrast with one shifted population;
  - we bound how much events differ;
  - we show the result does not depend on how the criterion is set;
  - we find no resolved step at the warming threshold in observations.

**What we report against our own expectations.** Every test is listed with its registration status, and
designs were committed before their data. Four results go against our expectations, and the manuscript
reports them as limits, not as footnotes:
- **Operational forecasts.** They under-predicted the shift after the SSWs of 1998–2021, but the bias did not
  recur after four later events.
- **Model-derived cold-risk probabilities.** They match 39 observed SSWs but over-predicted after twelve held-out
  SSWs of 1941–1958.
- **Event-to-event variation.** Event-conditioned responses differ substantially (18 model SSWs re-run as
  40-member ensembles: between-event variance about 80% of the within-ensemble variance). How much of that the
  stratosphere forces is not settled, and we state so.
- **Statistical forecasts.** A forecast conditioned on the continuous state improves on the fixed SSW rule by
  about 10%, with an interval that includes zero; calibrated dynamical ensembles, compared at the same issue
  dates, do best.
