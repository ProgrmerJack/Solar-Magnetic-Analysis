# Cover letter — Nature Geoscience, Article

[[AUTHORS: date, names, affiliations, corresponding author]]

Dear Editor,

We submit "Stratospheric warmings change the odds of downward outcomes, not the downward–non-downward contrast" for
consideration as an Article.

**The question.** When does an apparent separation between climate-impact classes tell us about the forcing,
and when does it mainly reflect how the outcomes were selected? Earth-system extremes are often sorted into
classes (El Niño flavours, weather regimes, climatic epochs), and a contrast between classes is read as a
difference in what drives them. Some typologies have survived scrutiny. Others have proved consistent with a
single regime or with stochastic variability (Stephenson et al., QJRMS 2004; Neukom et al., Nature 2019), or
reproducible as a continuum (Thual & Dewitte, Nature Geoscience 2023).

Sudden stratospheric warmings (SSWs) are a case with direct stakes. They precede weeks of cold, blocked weather
over Europe and Asia; in the UK alone they have been linked to about 620 extra deaths per event. Research and
forecasting sort them into the roughly two thirds whose influence "propagates downward" to the surface and the
rest. The label is defined from the surface outcome it is then used to explain, and its users recognise that
part of the class difference is built in by that definition. What has not been measured is how much: whether
an imposed stratospheric change alters the class contrast or only the class rate, and whether a null containing
no event reproduces the contrast and predicts held-out events.

**What we show.** We test the question by intervention.
- **Forcing changes the odds, not the contrast.** In an experiment that imposes the same observed SSW on every
  ensemble member in nine models, the share of downward outcomes rises from 45% to 84%, higher in all 36
  model–initialisation pairs, and the polar-cap annular mode shifts by 1.4 standard deviations. The contrast
  between downward and non-downward members changes by −0.03 [−0.12, +0.05] standard deviations (25 pairs; in
  the other 11 the forcing leaves too few non-downward members to form a contrast, which we show).
- **Observations follow without any model.** Event-free dates displaced by the SSW shift reproduce the observed
  "two thirds", 97% of the class contrast, and the class difference in northern-Eurasian temperature, a variable
  not used to classify (registered test; the 39 events cannot exclude a further 1.3 K difference).
- **The null is useful, not only consistent.** With the shift estimated from other winters, it predicts held-out
  events better than climatology (continuous ranked probability score gain 0.19 [0.07, 0.30]) and the label as
  well as the observed class rate.

**Why it matters beyond the stratosphere.** A class defined on the outcome it is meant to predict always shows
a contrast in that outcome. That is a general trap (Altman & Royston, BMJ 2006), and it also shapes attribution
debates: whether a particular SSW "caused" the February 2021 Texas cold outbreak is argued in both directions
(Cohen et al., Science 2021; Davis et al., Nature Communications 2022). Our results identify,
quantify and experimentally demonstrate a specific failure: reading a large class contrast as a large difference
in forcing. The classification stays legitimate for describing outcomes and as a forecast target (the rate does
respond to the forcing); what a realised label cannot carry is evidence of how strongly the stratosphere acted in
that event. We do not claim that distinct response types are absent, and we state what evidence would show them:
a class-related feature outside the defining outcome that a matched null cannot explain, or independently defined
groups of events responding differently to the same intervention.

**What is new relative to earlier work.**
- **Split and displaced vortices.** That typology was shown to differ little at the surface (Maycock &
  Hitchcock 2015). The downward label is different in kind: it is defined on the outcome.
- **White et al. (2019).** They showed, in one model, that the two classes already differ before onset. We go
  further:
  - we test the class statistics with an intervention that changes the forcing;
  - we reproduce the observed rate, the contrast and a regional class difference with one shifted population;
  - we show that this null predicts held-out winters;
  - we show the result does not depend on how the criterion is set.
- **Lu & Rao (2026)** document systematic contrasts between the classes without claiming their causes; we agree
  that description is legitimate and test the separate inference from contrast to forcing.

**What we report against our own expectations.** Every test is listed with its registration status, and
designs were committed before their data. Four results go against our expectations, and the manuscript
reports them as limits, not as footnotes:
- **Operational forecasts.** They under-predicted the shift after the SSWs of 1998–2021, but the bias did not
  recur after four later events.
- **Model-derived cold-risk probabilities.** They match 39 observed SSWs but over-predicted after twelve held-out
  SSWs of 1941–1958.
- **Event-to-event variation.** Event-conditioned responses differ substantially (18 model SSWs re-run as
  40-member ensembles: between-event variance about 80% of the within-ensemble variance). How much of that the
  stratosphere forces is not settled, and we state so.
- **Statistical forecasts.** A forecast conditioned on the continuous state improves on the fixed SSW rule by
  about 10%, with an interval that includes zero; calibrated dynamical ensembles, compared at the same issue
  dates, do best.
