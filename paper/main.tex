%  ABSTRACT  (~200 words)
% ============================================================
\begin{abstract}
Stratospheric sudden warming (SSW) events produce persistent surface weather anomalies, but whether they can measurably alter threshold-governed natural hazards is unknown. Here we report that natural dry slab avalanche counts in Switzerland decrease by two-thirds during SSW windows (14 of 16 events show suppression; geometric mean $\mathrm{RR} = 0.32$, 95\% CI $[0.26, 0.53]$; $d = -1.06$; permutation $P < 0.001$). Suppression begins before formal onset, coinciding with the precursor planetary-wave forcing that triggers each SSW. Norwegian and Utah archives directionally support the Swiss result; a Canadian archive shows the opposite sign, indicating regional dependence. Process-model diagnostics suggest that snowpack loading persists while natural-release triggers are selectively suppressed, consistent with weather-regime redistribution toward cold-dry conditions. No retrospective forecast skill is demonstrated (leave-one-out Brier skill score $= -0.95$), and the result requires prospective replication before causal interpretation. These findings establish an observational association between stratospheric variability and a threshold-governed natural hazard.
\end{abstract}

Stratospheric sudden warming events are among the strongest disturbances of the winter polar vortex and are followed by persistent surface anomalies including cold outbreaks, blocked circulation and precipitation shifts\cite{Matsuno1971,Baldwin2001,Kidston2015}. Those responses are well established for temperature and precipitation extremes---the February 2021 Texas cold-air outbreak, linked to a polar-vortex disruption, caused over 200 deaths and \$100~billion in damages\cite{Cohen2021}---but whether SSWs can substantially modulate threshold-governed geophysical hazards beyond meteorology remains unclear. Avalanches provide a stringent test because release depends on modest meteorological perturbations acting on a preconditioned snowpack, and because independent occurrence, forecast and process-model records are available\cite{Schweizer2003,Reuter2018}.

Any avalanche anomaly near SSW onset has at least two physically plausible components. One is a precursor response to the same enhanced planetary wave forcing that generates the SSW; the other is an additional post-onset contribution from downward coupling after the stratospheric wind reversal. Separating these components requires event timing, cross-region consistency and mechanism tests\cite{Polvani2004}.

Here we test whether SSW windows reorganize avalanche trigger environments strongly enough to alter natural dry slab occurrence. We focus on natural dry slabs because they respond directly to atmospheric forcing, whereas human-triggered releases also depend on exposure. We then ask whether the anomaly is better explained by wholesale snowpack stabilization or by threshold amplification, in which several natural release pathways are weakened simultaneously.

\section*{Results}

\noindent\textit{Analytical hierarchy and study origin.} This study evolved from an exploratory geomagnetic hypothesis to the SSW framework reported here; the effective multiple-testing burden from this trajectory is difficult to quantify, and readers should interpret all results as requiring prospective replication. The primary confirmatory test is the Swiss event-level sign test (14/16 events with $\mathrm{RR} < 1$; permutation $P < 0.001$). Multi-country comparisons, process-model diagnostics, mediation analyses and spatial gradient estimates are treated as exploratory consistency checks and are clearly labelled as such throughout. The SSW event ($n = 16$) is the inferential unit; region-day and station-day analyses are used descriptively. All 29 hypothesis tests are corrected with BH-FDR in a single family (Supplementary Table~7).

\subsection*{Natural dry slab avalanches decline during SSW windows}

We analysed daily counts of natural-trigger dry slab avalanches (size class $\geq$1) from the Swiss WSL/SLF database across 21 winters (1998/99--2018/19; Fig.~\ref{fig:study_area}). For each of 16 SSW events identified from the Butler et al. catalog\cite{Butler2015}, we compared observed counts in a $\pm$15-day window around onset with day-of-year-matched expectations from non-SSW winters. Fourteen of 16 events yield $\mathrm{RR} < 1$ (sign test $P = 0.002$; Fig.~\ref{fig:ssw_matched}). The geometric mean rate ratio is $\mathrm{RR} = 0.32$ (95\% CI $[0.26, 0.53]$, winter-blocked bootstrap; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap_comparison}), corresponding to a 68\% reduction in natural dry slab counts. The effect remains significant by paired $t$-test on $\log(\mathrm{RR})$ ($P < 0.001$), Wilcoxon signed-rank test ($P < 0.001$) and permutation testing ($P = 0.0005$), with a large effect size ($d = -1.06$).

The two contrary events occur during exceptional synoptic situations rather than under ordinary SSW conditions: the February 2018 split-vortex event with strong wind-slab loading and the January 2019 Alpine snowfall extreme. Even so, the core signal is not driven by any single event. All leave-one-out folds remain significant (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:loo}), excluding January 2019 strengthens the mean suppression (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:outlier}), and a 180-specification curve in the SI returns $\mathrm{RR} < 1$ for every analytical variant.

Timing helps distinguish mechanism from coincidence. Suppression is already present before the official wind reversal: in the pre-SSW interval ($-15$ to $-6$~d), 13 of 16 events show lower activity (median $\mathrm{RR} = 0.16$, $P = 0.011$), and suppression then persists through onset and post-onset windows (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:phases}; Fig.~\ref{fig:event_study}). Swiss and Norwegian lag profiles peak at $-13$ and $-12$~days, respectively, and are positively correlated across lags $-15$ to $+15$~days (Spearman $r = 0.62$, $P = 2.2 \times 10^{-4}$), indicating that the precursor structure is shared across independent countries and measurement systems. Because SSW events are preceded by enhanced upward planetary wave activity\cite{Polvani2004}, this pre-onset anomaly argues against a purely top-down interpretation.

\subsection*{Independent datasets show a shared large-scale signal}

Independent measurement systems support the same directional result. All four Norwegian SSW events show lower mean NVE danger than matched controls across ten inland mountain regions ($\Delta = -0.48$, $d = -0.67$; underpowered at the event level with $n = 4$, but directionality is consistent). All four SSW events in the Utah Avalanche Center record also show reduced dry slab activity (geometric mean $\mathrm{RR} = 0.34$; Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:utah}). A reconstructed M\'et\'eo-France BRA archive for 14 northern-Alpine massifs is directionally consistent ($\Delta = -0.15$, 95\% bootstrap CI $[-0.30, -0.01]$; three of four events negative), although extending to all 22 French massifs attenuates the signal ($\Delta = -0.08$, $P = 0.083$), with suppression concentrated in the northern Alps (Supplementary Table 26b--c). A post-2022 Avalanche Canada flexible-forecast panel shows the opposite sign at the national scale ($\Delta = +0.37$; Supplementary Table 26d), although Avalanche Qu\'ebec is strongly negative ($\Delta = -0.94$; Supplementary Table 26e), indicating within-country heterogeneity. We treat the Canadian archive as a boundary condition demonstrating regional dependence. Across Switzerland and Utah---the two direct occurrence-count datasets---18 of 20 event pairs decrease (random-effects meta-analysis: pooled $\Delta = -0.75$ aval.~day$^{-1}$, 95\% CI $[-1.33, -0.17]$, $P = 0.012$, $I^2 = 32\%$; Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:multi}; Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:multi_country}). Norwegian and French results are interpreted as directional consistency checks rather than standalone significance tests. Exploratory spatial analyses using EAWS data (only two SSW events) are reported in the SI.

\subsection*{Differential trigger response}

Swiss occurrence counts and Swiss danger ratings move in opposite directions. Re-analysed Swiss dry-avalanche danger levels increase in 11 of 14 SSW events even as natural dry slab counts fall. The differential trigger response is visible in the contrast between natural and human-triggered counts: natural counts decrease strongly (RR = 0.32) while human-triggered dry slabs decrease less (RR = 0.68, $P = 0.13$, non-significant), shifting the human-to-natural ratio 1.81-fold ($P = 0.058$; Supplementary Table~32). When all avalanche types are considered, human-triggered counts increase overall ($\mathrm{RR} = 1.45$; Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:human}), likely reflecting additional wind-slab formation from increased wind transport ($+22.7\%$), because wind slabs are predominantly released by human triggering on lee slopes\cite{Schweizer2003}. Austrian LAWIS incident data, dominated by human-triggered encounters, also increase during SSW windows. This count--rating dissociation indicates that SSW windows do not simply produce a safer snowpack.

SNOWPACK diagnostics provide a mechanistically plausible interpretation. Across 292,837 station-days from 130 Swiss stations, SSW windows show more loading (HN24 $+9.8\%$, SWE $+8.1\%$), greater persistent-weak-layer prevalence and lower modelled stability indices, conditions that should favour natural release. At the same time, the proximate natural-release triggers are suppressed: snow-surface warming events decrease by 57\%, rain-on-snow events by 29\%, and incoming shortwave radiation by 17.5\%. Independent Rutschblock tests show a small but statistically significant stability increase ($d = 0.10$, $P = 0.003$; Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:rutsch}). This reflects a well-known scale dependence in avalanche mechanics\cite{Schweizer2008}: point-scale structural instability can persist while landscape-scale trigger propagation is reduced under cold-dry conditions\cite{Reuter2018}. The count decline is therefore consistent with suppression of the trigger mechanisms that convert localised instability into landscape-scale natural release.

Large-scale circulation integrates the response better than local surface variables alone. Alpine SSW windows are colder and shifted toward cold-dry regimes (Fig.~\ref{fig:era5_composites}). At the daily level, a four-regime decomposition shows that SSW windows nearly double the frequency of cold-dry days while reducing warm-wet days. Within regimes, SSW and non-SSW days have near-identical avalanche rates in three of four cases, indicating that the association operates primarily through regime redistribution rather than within-regime rate changes. When weather regime and day-of-year are controlled simultaneously, the residual SSW effect is $\mathrm{IRR} = 0.89$ ($P = 0.06$; SI~Table~14)---not significant at conventional thresholds, but consistent with regime redistribution as the primary mediating pathway. At the event level, Z500 anomalies correlate with avalanche response ($r = 0.56$, $R^2 = 0.31$, $P = 0.024$), and events with stronger circulation reorganization show greater suppression (Extended Data Tables~\ref{tab:mechanism} and \ref{tab:synoptic}; Extended Data Figs.~\ref{fig:dose_response} and \ref{fig:mechanism_chain}). Regional Alpine Z500 substantially outperforms hemispheric AO and NAO indices ($R^2 < 0.01$ for both), consistent with the finding that SSW surface impacts are regionally mediated rather than hemispherically uniform\cite{Kretschmer2018}.

\subsection*{Robustness and confounding checks}

The suppression is specific to the trigger class that should be most sensitive to weather forcing. Natural dry slabs show the primary signal, whereas wet natural avalanches show an increase ($\mathrm{RR} = 1.62$, $P = 0.038$ nominally, non-significant after BH-FDR correction), consistent with spring rebound following cold-suppressed melt cycles (SI~Section~1). This divergence is hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Potential observer-effort bias is also inconsistent with the data: total avalanche reports increase during SSW windows, human-triggered counts across all avalanche types increase, and professional danger ratings rise. The decline is therefore selective to spontaneous dry-slab release rather than to access or reporting intensity.

Sensitivity tests point in the same direction. The signal survives leave-one-out analysis, changes little across reasonable day-of-year bandwidths and SSW window widths, and remains present for both displacement and split-vortex events (Extended Data Tables~\ref{tab:loo} and \ref{tab:sensitivity}). The full specification curve (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:spec_curve}), Bayesian analysis and alternative catalog tests are reported in the SI, but every one of the 180 analytical variants yields $\mathrm{RR} < 1$. When restricting to the post-2005 subperiod ($n = 9$ events), the effect direction is preserved ($\mathrm{RR} = 0.45$) but significance is lost ($P = 0.18$), consistent with reduced power from halving the sample rather than effect reversal (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}).

\subsection*{Weather--hazard amplification gap}

The composite near-surface temperature shift during SSW windows is $d_{\mathrm{weather}} = 0.69$, whereas the avalanche response is $d_{\mathrm{hazard}} = 1.06$---a gap qualitatively consistent with the simultaneous weakening of multiple partially independent trigger channels. An illustrative multi-trigger model (Supplementary Table~34) reproduces the observed RR with $k = 7$ channels, but is exactly identified with zero degrees of freedom and should be treated as a heuristic rather than an independent prediction.

\section*{Discussion}

We report that Swiss natural dry slab avalanche counts fall by about two-thirds during SSW windows---the first event-level evidence that stratospheric variability is associated with a discrete natural-hazard response. The signal is directionally consistent across independent occurrence and forecast systems in three countries, with a Canadian archive showing the opposite sign. The primary confirmatory result is the Swiss sign test (14/16, $P = 0.002$; permutation $P < 0.001$); all supporting analyses are exploratory.

\paragraph{Broader significance.} SSW-driven cold-air outbreaks have been linked to energy-grid failures\cite{Cohen2021}, excess mortality\cite{Gasparrini2015} and transport disruption, but these are continuous-response systems where hazard scales approximately linearly with temperature. Avalanches represent a qualitatively different case: a threshold-governed system in which release requires one or more trigger conditions to be satisfied. Whether the weather--hazard amplification gap documented here extends to other compound-hazard systems---such as atmospheric rivers modulating landslides\cite{Zscheischler2020}---remains an open question.

\paragraph{Regional circulation mediates the signal.} The null AO/NAO result ($R^2 < 0.01$) does not undermine the stratospheric framing; it clarifies it. Hemispheric annular-mode indices average over regional heterogeneity and are known to be poor predictors of local surface impacts\cite{Kretschmer2018}. Regional Alpine Z500 explains 31\% of event-level variance ($P = 0.024$). The residual SSW effect after controlling for weather regime ($\mathrm{IRR} = 0.89$, $P = 0.06$) does not reach significance at conventional thresholds; we cannot distinguish full from partial mediation with the present sample size.

\paragraph{Mechanistic interpretation and independent validation.} SNOWPACK diagnostics show that loading and persistent-weak-layer susceptibility remain elevated during SSW windows, while the proximate natural-release triggers are suppressed (warming $-57\%$, rain-on-snow $-29\%$, shortwave $-17.5\%$). Wind transport increases 22.7\%, consistent with the observed increase in human-triggered avalanches\cite{Schweizer2003}. The French BRA suppression ($\Delta = -0.15$; three of four events negative) provides cross-model validation: the S2M reanalysis chain\cite{Vernay2022} uses ARPEGE forcing and the Crocus snowpack model, entirely independent of the ERA5/SNOWPACK chain used for Swiss diagnostics. SAFRAN has been validated against 665 independent snow-depth measurements\cite{Durand2009}. The directional consistency of the French signal therefore tests the association across model families, not merely across political boundaries.

\paragraph{Common-cause timing.} Suppression begins before the formal wind reversal (pre-onset: 13/16 events, median $\mathrm{RR} = 0.16$, $P = 0.011$). Enhanced upward wave-activity flux consistently precedes SSW onset by 7--10~days\cite{Polvani2004}, and Swiss and Norwegian lag profiles both peak at $-12$ to $-13$~days, coinciding with this precursor forcing window. This temporal structure supports a common-cause interpretation: the same planetary-wave amplification that eventually produces the SSW simultaneously reorganises Alpine circulation.

\paragraph{Limitations.} (1)~The primary result rests on $n = 16$ events from 21 Swiss winters; the post-2005 subperiod ($n = 9$) preserves the effect direction ($\mathrm{RR} = 0.45$) but loses significance ($P = 0.18$), consistent with reduced statistical power (minimum detectable $d$ at 80\% power $= 0.75$ for $n = 16$ vs $1.01$ for $n = 9$) rather than effect reversal. (2)~Direct occurrence-count replication comes from Switzerland and Utah only ($n = 20$ event pairs); Norwegian and French supporting datasets use ordinal danger forecasts and are interpreted as directional checks. (3)~The Canadian archive shows the opposite sign, demonstrating regional dependence. (4)~The project evolved from an exploratory geomagnetic hypothesis to the SSW framework; the effective multiple-testing burden is difficult to quantify. (5)~The pipeline was executed by a single author; the open code repository and REVIEWER\_INDEX enable post-publication verification but do not substitute for independent team replication. (6)~No retrospective forecast skill is demonstrated (LOO BSS $= -0.95$); the threshold-amplification model is exactly identified with zero degrees of freedom and requires prospective replication.

These results establish an observational association between stratospheric circulation variability and a threshold-governed natural hazard. The convergence across independent measurement systems and the mechanistic consistency of trigger suppression support the association, while the limitations above constrain the strength of causal inference. Prospective validation against future SSW events is the most important next step.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Avalanche and hazard datasets}

The primary outcome was the Swiss WSL/SLF daily count of natural-trigger dry slab avalanches of size class $\geq$1 across 21 winters (1998/99--2018/19; 7,518~days). We selected natural dry slabs because they respond directly to atmospheric forcing, whereas human-triggered avalanches also depend on recreation patterns and access. Supporting datasets were the Utah Avalanche Center dry slab record (2012--2025), Norwegian NVE avalanche danger forecasts from ten inland mountain regions (2017/18--2022/23), reconstructed M\'et\'eo-France BRA danger bulletins for 14 northern-Alpine massifs (2016/17--2022/23; 6,839 massif-days), a post-2022 Avalanche Canada flexible-forecast panel aggregated from dated metadata and matching forecast polygons (189 forecast dates; 4,661 region-days), EAWS daily maximum danger levels for approximately 100 Alpine regions across Austria, Switzerland, France, Italy and Germany (2011--2015), Swiss EnviDat reanalysed regional danger levels and human-triggered avalanche counts, ALBINA danger levels for Tyrol, South Tyrol and Trentino (2018--2024), Austrian LAWIS incident records (1992--2024), Swiss Rutschblock tests, and SNOWPACK simulations from 130 Swiss stations. Detailed data access routes, variable definitions and additional regional summaries are provided in the SI.

\subsection*{SSW identification and matched-control design}

SSW events were identified from the Butler et al. catalog\cite{Butler2015} as reversals of the 10~hPa 60$^\circ$N zonal-mean zonal wind from westerly to easterly. Sixteen events fall within the Swiss study period, four each within the Utah, Norwegian and French BRA periods, and two within the post-2022 Avalanche Canada flexible-forecast period.

For each event we computed the rate ratio $\mathrm{RR} = O / E$, where $O$ is the observed natural dry slab count in the $\pm$15-day window around onset and $E$ is the day-of-year-matched expectation. The expected value was obtained from the same day of year $\pm$3~days across non-SSW winters only, summed over the event window. This design controls seasonality by construction and prevents SSW contamination of the reference climatology. For ordinal danger data we compared SSW-window and matched-control distributions rather than counts; the Avalanche Canada extension used area-weighted daily national summaries because flexible forecast regions change through time.

\subsection*{Circulation, snowpack and trigger diagnostics}

ERA5 daily fields for the Swiss Alps (2~m temperature, total precipitation, snowfall, rainfall partition, wind speed and surface-radiation terms) were used to diagnose Alpine surface anomalies during SSW windows. The ERA5 surface analysis covers 2004--2013, the period for which SNOWPACK model runs from the WSL/SLF EnviDat archive provide complete station-level forcing and output across all 130 automatic weather stations; 11 of 16 SSW events fall within this window (selection criterion: completeness of SNOWPACK forcing, not outcome). Northern Hemisphere sea-level pressure, 500~hPa geopotential height and 850~hPa zonal wind from NCEP reanalysis were used to quantify large-scale circulation anomalies across the full 1998--2019 study period. SNOWPACK output provided loading, stability and persistent-weak-layer diagnostics, and daily trigger-frequency metrics were derived from station-level warming, rain-on-snow and radiative conditions. Full variable lists, station filtering and supporting analyses are reported in the SI.

\subsection*{Independent cross-model validation}

The French BRA danger-level analysis provides methodologically independent evidence for the mechanistic pathway. The S2M reanalysis chain\cite{Vernay2022} uses SAFRAN meteorological analysis (driven by the ARPEGE numerical weather prediction model and assimilating observations from a dense French Alpine station network) coupled to the Crocus snowpack model and the MEPRA avalanche hazard module. This chain is entirely independent of the ERA5 global reanalysis and the SNOWPACK model used for Swiss diagnostics: different meteorological forcing, different station networks, different snowpack physics and different hazard indices. SAFRAN has been validated against 665 independent snow depth measurements across 130 French stations with correlation coefficients exceeding 0.9 for seasonal snow depth\cite{Durand2009}. The directional consistency of French BRA suppression with the Swiss occurrence-count result therefore tests whether the SSW--avalanche association generalises across model families, not merely across political boundaries.

\subsection*{Statistics}

Primary inference used the 16 event-level Swiss rate ratios and was assessed with an exact sign test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test on $\log(\mathrm{RR})$, one-sample $t$-test on $\log(\mathrm{RR})$ and a permutation test using 10,000 random winter-date sets. Bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals for the geometric mean RR were obtained from 10,000 event resamples; a winter-blocked bootstrap (resampling entire winter seasons rather than individual events) yields a slightly wider CI ([0.26, 0.53] versus [0.26, 0.51] standard), confirming that within-winter dependence does not inflate inference (Supplementary Table 34c). Norwegian, French BRA, Avalanche Canada, EAWS, ALBINA, LAWIS and Rutschblock comparisons used matched-control danger comparisons with Mann--Whitney, sign or Wilcoxon tests as appropriate. Pearson correlation and small multivariable models were used for event-level circulation predictors. Primary hypothesis tests were corrected with the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:fdr}), and extended sensitivity, Bayesian and mediation analyses are provided in the SI. Power considerations for the Swiss, Norwegian and Utah event-level analyses are reported in Supplementary Table 26; Norway and Utah are interpreted as directional external checks rather than standalone null-hypothesis tests.

Swiss avalanche data are available from WSL/SLF upon request. Re-analysed Swiss regional danger levels, human-triggered avalanche rates, Rutschblock test results, and SNOWPACK process-model output (weather, stability indices, persistent weak layer diagnostics) are available from the WSL/SLF EnviDat repository (\url{https://www.envidat.ch}). EAWS danger-level data are available via avalanches.org. ALBINA danger-level data are available via \url{https://avalanche.report}. Utah data are publicly available from the Utah Avalanche Center. Norwegian NVE data are available via their public API. French BRA bulletins are publicly available from the M\'et\'eo-France archive (\url{https://donneespubliques.meteofrance.fr/donnees_libres/Pdf/BRA/}). \caption{\textbf{Causal diagram for the SSW--avalanche association.}
The \textbf{primary pathway} (green arrows): enhanced planetary wave forcing simultaneously triggers SSW events and reorganises tropospheric circulation through Z500 depression ($d = -0.83$, $P < 10^{-73}$), producing a weather regime shift toward cold-dry conditions ($\chi^2 = 83.5$, $P = 5 \times 10^{-18}$).
