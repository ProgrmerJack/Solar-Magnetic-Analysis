%  ABSTRACT  (~200 words)
% ============================================================
\begin{abstract}
Threshold-governed hazards can amplify modest circulation shifts, but event-level tests remain rare. Here we show that natural dry slab avalanches in Switzerland decrease by two-thirds during stratospheric sudden warming (SSW) windows (14/16 events; geometric mean $\mathrm{RR} = 0.32$, 95\% CI $[0.26, 0.53]$; $d = -1.06$), with suppression beginning before formal onset. Norwegian, French and Utah archives support the same directional signal. Process-model evidence indicates a loaded-gun state: snowpack loading persists while natural-release triggers---surface warming, rain-on-snow, shortwave radiation---are selectively suppressed. A 180-variant specification curve retains $\mathrm{RR} < 1$ throughout (permutation $P < 0.001$), though no retrospective forecast skill is demonstrated (LOO Brier skill score $= -0.95$). The suppression is mediated by weather-regime redistribution ($\mathrm{IRR} = 0.89$, $P = 0.06$ after regime control) rather than a direct stratospheric pathway. These results identify avalanche hazard as a case of stratospheric modulation of threshold-governed surface processes, where simultaneous weakening of multiple trigger channels produces super-linear hazard reduction.
\end{abstract}

Stratospheric sudden warming events are among the strongest disturbances of the winter polar vortex and are followed by persistent surface anomalies including cold outbreaks, blocked circulation and precipitation shifts\cite{Matsuno1971,Baldwin2001,Kidston2015}. Those responses are well established for temperature and precipitation extremes---the February 2021 Texas cold-air outbreak, linked to a polar-vortex disruption, caused over 200 deaths and \$100~billion in damages\cite{Cohen2021}---but whether SSWs can substantially modulate threshold-governed geophysical hazards beyond meteorology remains unclear. Avalanches provide a stringent test because release depends on modest meteorological perturbations acting on a preconditioned snowpack, and because independent occurrence, forecast and process-model records are available\cite{Schweizer2003,Reuter2018}. With approximately 100 fatalities per year in the European Alps alone\cite{Techel2016}, avalanches represent the most lethal weather-sensitive natural hazard in mountain regions.

Any avalanche anomaly near SSW onset has at least two physically plausible components. One is a precursor response to the same enhanced planetary wave forcing that generates the SSW; the other is an additional post-onset contribution from downward coupling after the stratospheric wind reversal. Separating these components requires event timing, cross-region consistency and mechanism tests\cite{Polvani2004}.

Here we test whether SSW windows reorganize avalanche trigger environments strongly enough to alter natural dry slab occurrence. We focus on natural dry slabs because they respond directly to atmospheric forcing, whereas human-triggered releases also depend on exposure. We then ask whether the anomaly is better explained by wholesale snowpack stabilization or by threshold amplification, in which several natural release pathways are weakened simultaneously.

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Natural dry slab avalanches decline during SSW windows}

We analysed daily counts of natural-trigger dry slab avalanches (size class $\geq$1) from the Swiss WSL/SLF database across 21 winters (1998/99--2018/19; Fig.~\ref{fig:study_area}). For each of 16 SSW events identified from the Butler et al. catalog\cite{Butler2015}, we compared observed counts in a $\pm$15-day window around onset with day-of-year-matched expectations from non-SSW winters. Fourteen of 16 events yield $\mathrm{RR} < 1$ (sign test $P = 0.002$; Fig.~\ref{fig:ssw_matched}). The geometric mean rate ratio is $\mathrm{RR} = 0.32$ (95\% CI $[0.26, 0.53]$, winter-blocked bootstrap; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap_comparison}), corresponding to a 68\% reduction in natural dry slab counts. The effect remains significant by paired $t$-test on $\log(\mathrm{RR})$ ($P < 0.001$), Wilcoxon signed-rank test ($P < 0.001$) and permutation testing ($P = 0.0005$), with a large effect size ($d = -1.06$).

The two contrary events occur during exceptional synoptic situations rather than under ordinary SSW conditions: the February 2018 split-vortex event with strong wind-slab loading and the January 2019 Alpine snowfall extreme. Even so, the core signal is not driven by any single event. All leave-one-out folds remain significant (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:loo}), excluding January 2019 strengthens the mean suppression (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:outlier}), and a 180-specification curve in the SI returns $\mathrm{RR} < 1$ for every analytical variant.

Timing helps distinguish mechanism from coincidence. Suppression is already present before the official wind reversal: in the pre-SSW interval ($-15$ to $-6$~d), 13 of 16 events show lower activity (median $\mathrm{RR} = 0.16$, $P = 0.011$), and suppression then persists through onset and post-onset windows (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:phases}; Fig.~\ref{fig:event_study}). Swiss and Norwegian lag profiles peak at $-13$ and $-12$~days, respectively, and are positively correlated across lags $-15$ to $+15$~days (Spearman $r = 0.62$, $P = 2.2 \times 10^{-4}$), indicating that the precursor structure is shared across independent countries and measurement systems. Because SSW events are preceded by enhanced upward planetary wave activity\cite{Polvani2004}, this pre-onset anomaly argues against a purely top-down interpretation.

\subsection*{Independent datasets show a shared large-scale signal}

Independent measurement systems support the same directional result. Norwegian NVE danger forecasts from ten inland mountain regions are lower during SSW windows than in day-of-year-matched controls ($\Delta = -0.48$, $d = -0.67$, Mann--Whitney $P < 10^{-6}$); all four Norwegian SSW events show lower mean danger, and 20 of 23 event--region comparisons decrease. All four SSW events in the Utah Avalanche Center record also show reduced dry slab activity (geometric mean $\mathrm{RR} = 0.34$; Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:utah}). A reconstructed M\'et\'eo-France BRA archive for 14 northern-Alpine massifs is directionally consistent: matched-window danger is lower during SSW windows ($\Delta = -0.15$, 95\% bootstrap CI $[-0.30, -0.01]$), and 37 of 56 event--massif comparisons decrease (sign test $P = 0.011$; Wilcoxon $P = 0.019$), although one of the four French events runs positive (Supplementary Table 26b). Extending the archive to all 22 available French Alpine massifs attenuates the pooled French effect ($\Delta = -0.08$, sign test $P = 0.083$), with suppression concentrated in the northern French Alps while the southern French Alps are near-null to slightly positive (Supplementary Table 26c). A post-2022 Avalanche Canada flexible-forecast panel points the other way at the national scale: area-weighted danger is higher during the 2023 and 2024 SSW windows than in day-of-year-matched controls ($\Delta = +0.37$, 95\% bootstrap CI $[0.23, 0.51]$; 18 of 61 daily pairs decrease; Supplementary Table 26d). However, owner-stratified analysis shows that this Canadian aggregate is not spatially uniform: the area-dominant Avalanche Canada owner ($\sim$97\% of mean daily forecast area) and Parks Canada remain positive, whereas Avalanche Qu\'ebec is strongly negative ($\Delta = -0.94$, 53 of 61 daily pairs decrease; Supplementary Table 26e). We therefore treat the Canadian archive as a structured boundary condition rather than as a monolithic supportive or adverse replicate. Across the two direct occurrence-count datasets, 18 of 20 event pairs decrease, and a random-effects meta-analysis of Swiss and Utah mean-difference estimates yields a pooled effect of $-0.75$ avalanches day$^{-1}$ (95\% CI $[-1.33, -0.17]$; $P = 0.012$; $I^2 = 32\%$). Across Switzerland, Norway and Utah, 22 of 24 event-level comparisons show decreased avalanche activity (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:multi}; Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:multi_country}).

The Alpine response is spatially structured rather than uniform. Exploratory EAWS data from 22 forecast centres across five countries suggest a north--south gradient (southern centres suppressed, northern centres enhanced; $r = 0.69$, $P = 0.0004$; Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:eaws_map}), but this rests on only two SSW events (January 2012 and January 2013) from 142,465 region-days, so the spatial pattern is preliminary and requires confirmation as the EAWS record lengthens. Independent ALBINA bulletins broadly reproduce the north--south divergence, though Italian South Tyrol and Trentino show danger \emph{increases} ($d = +0.40$) rather than decreases, likely reflecting event-to-event variability (Supplementary Table~19). The gradient direction (Austrian suppression stronger than Italian) is preserved across archives, but the absolute sign is event-dependent. Together with the multi-country occurrence-count evidence, these spatial patterns indicate that SSW-associated avalanche anomalies are a regional circulation response, not a local Swiss artefact, whose sign depends on how each SSW episode projects onto Alpine weather.

\subsection*{Trigger suppression produces a loaded-gun state}

Swiss occurrence counts and Swiss danger ratings move in opposite directions. Re-analysed Swiss dry-avalanche danger levels increase in 11 of 14 SSW events even as natural dry slab counts fall. The loaded-gun signature emerges in the differential trigger response: while both natural and human-triggered dry slab counts decrease, natural counts decrease far more (RR = 0.32) than human-triggered dry slabs (RR = 0.68, $P = 0.13$, non-significant), shifting the human-to-natural ratio 1.81-fold ($P = 0.058$; Supplementary Table~32). When all avalanche types are considered, human-triggered counts increase overall ($\mathrm{RR} = 1.45$; Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:human}), reflecting additional wind-slab and wet-slab human triggers. Austrian LAWIS incident data, which are dominated by human-triggered encounters, also increase during SSW windows. This count--rating dissociation indicates that SSW windows do not simply produce a safer snowpack.

SNOWPACK diagnostics resolve the paradox. Across 292,837 station-days from 130 Swiss stations, SSW windows show more loading (HN24 $+9.8\%$, SWE $+8.1\%$), greater persistent-weak-layer prevalence and lower modelled stability indices, conditions that should favour natural release. At the same time, the proximate natural-release triggers are suppressed: snow-surface warming events decrease by 57\%, rain-on-snow events by 29\%, and incoming shortwave radiation by 17.5\%. Wind transport increases 22.7\%, consistent with the observed increase in all-type human-triggered avalanches ($\mathrm{RR} = 1.45$; Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:human}), because wind slabs are predominantly released by human triggering on lee slopes rather than by spontaneous natural mechanisms\cite{Schweizer2003}. Independent Rutschblock tests show a small but statistically significant stability increase ($d = 0.10$, $P = 0.003$; Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:rutsch}). This seemingly contradictory result reflects a well-known scale dependence in avalanche mechanics: Rutschblock tests measure point-scale structural failure propensity at a specific slope location, whereas natural dry slab release requires landscape-scale trigger propagation across heterogeneous terrain\cite{Schweizer2008}. In SSW windows, weak layers can persist or worsen (SNOWPACK) while slab-scale bridging over spatial variability increases under cold-dry conditions\cite{Reuter2018}, so the landscape-scale probability of spontaneous initiation decreases even as localised structural instability is maintained. The two-thirds count decline therefore reflects suppression of the trigger mechanisms that convert localised instability into landscape-scale natural release.

Large-scale circulation integrates the response better than local surface variables alone. Alpine SSW windows are colder and shifted toward cold-dry regimes (Fig.~\ref{fig:era5_composites}). At the daily level, a four-regime decomposition shows that SSW windows nearly double the frequency of cold-dry days while reducing warm-wet days. Within regimes, SSW and non-SSW days have near-identical avalanche rates in three of four cases, indicating that the suppression operates primarily through regime redistribution rather than within-regime rate changes. When weather regime and day-of-year are controlled simultaneously, the residual SSW effect is $\mathrm{IRR} = 0.89$ ($P = 0.06$; SI~Table~14), consistent with the association operating \emph{through} regime redistribution with at most a modest residual. At the event level, Z500 anomalies correlate with avalanche response ($r = 0.56$, $R^2 = 0.31$), Z500 together with SLP explains 41\% of the event-to-event variance, and events with stronger circulation reorganization are much more suppressed than weakly organized cases (Extended Data Tables~\ref{tab:mechanism} and \ref{tab:synoptic}; Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:dose_response}). Regional Alpine Z500 substantially outperforms hemispheric AO and NAO indices ($R^2 < 0.01$ for both), consistent with the finding that SSW surface impacts are regionally mediated rather than hemispherically uniform\cite{Kretschmer2018}. Event-to-event heterogeneity therefore reflects how each SSW projects onto Alpine circulation, not a simple one-number intensity scale (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism_chain}). We therefore interpret the SSW window as a loaded-gun state: loading and weak layers persist, but the surface-energy triggers that normally release natural dry slabs are selectively suppressed.

\subsection*{Robustness and confounding checks}

The suppression is specific to the trigger class that should be most sensitive to weather forcing. Natural dry slabs show the primary signal, whereas wet natural avalanches show an increase ($\mathrm{RR} = 1.62$, $P = 0.038$ nominally, non-significant after BH-FDR correction), consistent with spring rebound following cold-suppressed melt cycles (SI~Section~1). This divergence is hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Potential observer-effort bias is also inconsistent with the data: total avalanche reports increase during SSW windows, human-triggered counts across all avalanche types increase, and professional danger ratings rise. The decline is therefore selective to spontaneous dry-slab release rather than to access or reporting intensity.

Sensitivity tests point in the same direction. The signal survives leave-one-out analysis, changes little across reasonable day-of-year bandwidths and SSW window widths, and remains present for both displacement and split-vortex events (Extended Data Tables~\ref{tab:loo} and \ref{tab:sensitivity}). The full specification curve (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:spec_curve}), Bayesian analysis and alternative catalog tests are reported in the SI, but every one of the 180 analytical variants yields $\mathrm{RR} < 1$. When restricting to the post-2005 subperiod ($n = 9$ events), the effect direction is preserved ($\mathrm{RR} = 0.45$) but significance is lost ($P = 0.18$), consistent with reduced power from halving the sample rather than effect reversal (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}).

\subsection*{Threshold amplification links weather shifts to hazard response}

The weather anomaly is modest compared with the hazard response. In ERA5, the composite near-surface temperature shift corresponds to $d_{\mathrm{weather}} = 0.69$, whereas the Swiss avalanche response is $d_{\mathrm{hazard}} = 1.06$. A multi-trigger threshold framework explains this gap qualitatively. If natural dry slab release can be initiated through any of several partially independent trigger channels, then simultaneously weakening all of them increases the probability of no release super-linearly. An illustrative model with an effective seven-channel trigger system reproduces the empirical suppression ($\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{pred}} = 0.313$ versus $\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{obs}} = 0.32$; Supplementary Table 34), but this model is exactly identified ($k$ and $\theta$ fitted to match the observed RR and $d$) and therefore constitutes a consistency demonstration, not an independent prediction. Leave-one-out cross-validation shows that the optimal channel count is stable across events (median $k = 7$, range 6--15, mean $8.2 \pm 2.9$; Supplementary Table 34b), with LOO predicted RR spanning 0.285--0.367; the qualitative conclusion of super-linear amplification is robust but the quantitative amplification factor carries substantial uncertainty from $k$. Crucially, the predicted RR is nearly invariant to inter-channel correlations: Monte Carlo simulations show that even under high equicorrelation ($\rho = 0.6$--$0.8$, effective $k \approx 1.2$--$1.5$), the predicted RR remains 0.31--0.33 because correlations reduce both baseline and SSW-window trigger probabilities proportionally (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:corr_sensitivity}). The exact channel count is model-dependent, but the qualitative inference is not: modest circulation-driven changes in trigger availability can generate a much larger hazard response when multiple release pathways are suppressed at once.

\section*{Discussion}

We find that Swiss natural dry slab avalanche counts fall by about two-thirds during SSW windows---the first event-level evidence that stratospheric variability is associated with a discrete natural-hazard response. The signal survives several independent forecast and occurrence systems, reproduces a coherent Alpine spatial pattern, and admits a physically constrained mechanism.

\paragraph{Broader significance.} SSW-driven cold-air outbreaks have been linked to energy-grid failures\cite{Cohen2021}, excess mortality\cite{Gasparrini2015} and transport disruption, but these are continuous-response systems where hazard scales approximately linearly with temperature. Avalanches represent a qualitatively different case: a threshold-governed system in which multiple release pathways must be satisfied simultaneously. When several trigger channels are weakened at once, the probability of no release increases super-linearly---scaling as $[\Phi(\theta - d_{\mathrm{weather}})/\Phi(\theta)]^k$ for $k$ independent channels. This amplification principle is general: any persistent atmospheric state that simultaneously affects multiple trigger channels should produce analogous amplified responses. Atmospheric rivers modulating landslides through rainfall, soil moisture and snowmelt\cite{Zscheischler2020}, or blocking events affecting wildfire through temperature, humidity and wind, are natural candidates for testing this framework.

\paragraph{Regional circulation mediates the signal.} The null AO/NAO result ($R^2 < 0.01$) does not undermine the stratospheric framing; it clarifies it. Hemispheric annular-mode indices average over regional heterogeneity and are known to be poor predictors of local surface impacts\cite{Kretschmer2018}. Regional Alpine Z500 explains 31\% of event-level variance ($P = 0.024$), and the residual SSW effect after controlling for weather regime is $\mathrm{IRR} = 0.89$ ($P = 0.06$; SI~Table~14)---an at-most 11\% additional suppression beyond what regime redistribution alone would predict, consistent with the association operating \emph{through} regime redistribution rather than via an independent stratospheric pathway. SSW events have a defined lifecycle with predictable timing\cite{Sigmond2013}, and the pre-onset signal demonstrates that the same planetary-wave episodes that trigger SSWs simultaneously reorganise surface weather.

\paragraph{Mechanistic attribution.} The trigger-suppression mechanism is supported by convergent evidence across independent systems. SNOWPACK diagnostics show that loading and persistent-weak-layer susceptibility remain elevated during SSW windows, while the proximate natural-release triggers are selectively suppressed. Wind transport increases 22.7\%, but this is mechanistically consistent with the observed \emph{increase} in human-triggered avalanches ($\mathrm{RR} = 1.45$) because wind-slab formation enhances human-triggerable instability on lee slopes while cold-dry conditions suppress spontaneous release\cite{Schweizer2003}. Rutschblock field tests show a small stability increase ($d = 0.10$, $P = 0.003$), consistent with the multi-scale interpretation: point-scale structural instability persists while landscape-scale natural-release triggers are removed. The French BRA suppression ($\Delta = -0.15$, sign test $P = 0.011$; Wilcoxon $P = 0.019$) provides convergent evidence from a methodologically independent model chain: the S2M reanalysis (SAFRAN-Crocus-MEPRA\cite{Vernay2022}) uses ARPEGE meteorological forcing and dense French station networks, entirely independent of the ERA5-SNOWPACK system used for Swiss diagnostics.

\paragraph{Common-cause timing.} The pre-onset anomaly is critical. Suppression begins before the formal wind reversal (pre-onset: 13/16 events, median $\mathrm{RR} = 0.16$, $P = 0.011$), demonstrating that the avalanche response is embedded in the precursor planetary-wave episode rather than driven by post-onset downward coupling alone. Enhanced upward wave-activity flux consistently precedes SSW onset by 7--10~days\cite{Polvani2004}, and Swiss and Norwegian lag profiles both peak at $-12$ to $-13$~days, coinciding with this precursor forcing window. This temporal structure supports a common-cause interpretation: the same planetary-wave amplification that eventually produces the SSW simultaneously reorganises Alpine circulation and trigger environments.

\paragraph{Implications and limits.} The operational implications are regionally dependent and asymmetric: natural release risk decreases during SSW windows in the Swiss/western-Alpine zone, yet human-triggered risk increases, creating a loaded-gun state. In the northern Alps (Bavaria, Upper Austria), SSW windows instead enhance avalanche danger, so the operational takeaway differs by region. No retrospective forecast skill is demonstrated (LOO BSS $= -0.95$; in-sample ROC AUC of 0.85 does not translate to calibrated probabilistic forecasts), and the threshold model is exactly identified with zero degrees of freedom; prospective validation is required before any operational application.

Direct occurrence-count replication comes from Switzerland and Utah ($n = 20$ event pairs), whereas Norwegian and French supporting datasets use ordinal danger forecasts. The EAWS geographic gradient is an exploratory result from only two SSW events; the spatial structure is physically coherent but requires confirmation from additional events. The Canadian flexible-forecast extension reinforces that generalization depends on regional circulation geometry rather than a universal sign. The project evolved from an exploratory geomagnetic hypothesis to the SSW framework; the effective multiple-testing burden is difficult to quantify, and the result requires prospective replication. The pipeline was executed by a single author; the code repository and REVIEWER\_INDEX enable post-publication verification.

Even with these limits, the convergence across independent measurement systems, the mechanistic consistency of trigger suppression across scales, and the structured spatial pattern argue that SSW-associated planetary-wave episodes reorganise avalanche trigger environments---a finding that extends stratosphere-surface coupling research into the domain of threshold-governed natural hazards.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Avalanche and hazard datasets}

The primary outcome was the Swiss WSL/SLF daily count of natural-trigger dry slab avalanches of size class $\geq$1 across 21 winters (1998/99--2018/19; 7,518~days). We selected natural dry slabs because they respond directly to atmospheric forcing, whereas human-triggered avalanches also depend on recreation patterns and access. Supporting datasets were the Utah Avalanche Center dry slab record (2012--2025), Norwegian NVE avalanche danger forecasts from ten inland mountain regions (2017/18--2022/23), reconstructed M\'et\'eo-France BRA danger bulletins for 14 northern-Alpine massifs (2016/17--2022/23; 6,839 massif-days), a post-2022 Avalanche Canada flexible-forecast panel aggregated from dated metadata and matching forecast polygons (189 forecast dates; 4,661 region-days), EAWS daily maximum danger levels for approximately 100 Alpine regions across Austria, Switzerland, France, Italy and Germany (2011--2015), Swiss EnviDat reanalysed regional danger levels and human-triggered avalanche counts, ALBINA danger levels for Tyrol, South Tyrol and Trentino (2018--2024), Austrian LAWIS incident records (1992--2024), Swiss Rutschblock tests, and SNOWPACK simulations from 130 Swiss stations. Detailed data access routes, variable definitions and additional regional summaries are provided in the SI.

\subsection*{SSW identification and matched-control design}

SSW events were identified from the Butler et al. catalog\cite{Butler2015} as reversals of the 10~hPa 60$^\circ$N zonal-mean zonal wind from westerly to easterly. Sixteen events fall within the Swiss study period, four each within the Utah, Norwegian and French BRA periods, and two within the post-2022 Avalanche Canada flexible-forecast period.

For each event we computed the rate ratio $\mathrm{RR} = O / E$, where $O$ is the observed natural dry slab count in the $\pm$15-day window around onset and $E$ is the day-of-year-matched expectation. The expected value was obtained from the same day of year $\pm$3~days across non-SSW winters only, summed over the event window. This design controls seasonality by construction and prevents SSW contamination of the reference climatology. For ordinal danger data we compared SSW-window and matched-control distributions rather than counts; the Avalanche Canada extension used area-weighted daily national summaries because flexible forecast regions change through time.

\subsection*{Circulation, snowpack and trigger diagnostics}

ERA5 daily fields for the Swiss Alps (2~m temperature, total precipitation, snowfall, rainfall partition, wind speed and surface-radiation terms) were used to diagnose Alpine surface anomalies during SSW windows. The ERA5 surface analysis covers 2004--2013, the period for which SNOWPACK model runs from the WSL/SLF EnviDat archive provide complete station-level forcing and output across all 130 automatic weather stations; 11 of 16 SSW events fall within this window (selection criterion: completeness of SNOWPACK forcing, not outcome). Northern Hemisphere sea-level pressure, 500~hPa geopotential height and 850~hPa zonal wind from NCEP reanalysis were used to quantify large-scale circulation anomalies across the full 1998--2019 study period. SNOWPACK output provided loading, stability and persistent-weak-layer diagnostics, and daily trigger-frequency metrics were derived from station-level warming, rain-on-snow and radiative conditions. Full variable lists, station filtering and supporting analyses are reported in the SI.

\subsection*{Statistics}

Primary inference used the 16 event-level Swiss rate ratios and was assessed with an exact sign test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test on $\log(\mathrm{RR})$, one-sample $t$-test on $\log(\mathrm{RR})$ and a permutation test using 10,000 random winter-date sets. Bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals for the geometric mean RR were obtained from 10,000 event resamples; a winter-blocked bootstrap (resampling entire winter seasons rather than individual events) yields a slightly wider CI ([0.26, 0.53] versus [0.26, 0.51] standard), confirming that within-winter dependence does not inflate inference (Supplementary Table 34c). Norwegian, French BRA, Avalanche Canada, EAWS, ALBINA, LAWIS and Rutschblock comparisons used matched-control danger comparisons with Mann--Whitney, sign or Wilcoxon tests as appropriate. Pearson correlation and small multivariable models were used for event-level circulation predictors. Primary hypothesis tests were corrected with the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure (Extended Data Table~\ref{tab:fdr}), and extended sensitivity, Bayesian and mediation analyses are provided in the SI. Power considerations for the Swiss, Norwegian and Utah event-level analyses are reported in Supplementary Table 26; Norway and Utah are interpreted as directional external checks rather than standalone null-hypothesis tests.

Re-analysed Swiss regional danger levels, human-triggered avalanche rates, Rutschblock test results, and SNOWPACK process-model output (weather, stability indices, persistent weak layer diagnostics) are available from the WSL/SLF EnviDat repository (\url{https://www.envidat.ch}). EAWS danger-level data are available via avalanches.org. ALBINA danger-level data are available via \url{https://avalanche.report}. Utah data are publicly available from the Utah Avalanche Center. Norwegian NVE data are available via their public API. French BRA bulletins are publicly available from the M\'et\'eo-France archive (\url{https://donneespubliques.meteofrance.fr/donnees_libres/Pdf/BRA/}). \caption{\textbf{Causal diagram for the SSW--avalanche association.}
The \textbf{primary pathway} (green arrows): enhanced planetary wave forcing simultaneously triggers SSW events and reorganises tropospheric circulation through Z500 depression ($d = -0.83$, $P < 10^{-73}$), producing a weather regime shift toward cold-dry conditions ($\chi^2 = 83.5$, $P = 5 \times 10^{-18}$). SSW onset therefore serves as a detectable \emph{marker} of this broader wave-forced episode rather than as a direct cause. Within each regime, SSW and non-SSW days show near-identical avalanche rates ($\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{within}} = 0.98$--$1.16$ in three of four regimes; warm-dry residual attributable to seasonal confounding), supporting the interpretation that suppression is predominantly mediated by the regime shift. The \textbf{de-emphasized pathways} (red arrows): annular modes are not supported as dominant mediators ($R^2 < 0.01$ for both AO and NAO). Rutschblock tests show only a small field-scale stability increase ($P = 0.003$), insufficient to explain the count decline.}
